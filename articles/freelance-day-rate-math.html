The day-rate math most freelancers never do

Most solo professionals price by vibes — a number that sounds fair, said with a question mark. Here's the ten-minute arithmetic that replaces the vibes, using a worked example you can copy.

Step 1 — what the year must produce

income target $85,000
+ overheads $4,000 (software, gear, insurance, accounting)
= required revenue $89,000

Tax comes out of this per your own situation — hold your percentage from every payment; ask your accountant for the number.

Step 2 — how many days you can actually bill

This is where everyone lies to themselves. You will not bill 260 days.

52 weeks − 5 weeks off = 47 weeks
47 × 5 days = 235 working days
− ~33% non-billable (sales, admin, email, learning)
= ≈ 157 billable days

A third of a freelance week disappears into finding work and running the business. Budgeting 220+ billable days is how a good year still feels broke.

Step 3 — floor rate vs market rate

floor = $89,000 ÷ 157 = $567/day ← survival, never quote below
market = floor × 1.4 = $795/day ← what you actually charge

The 1.3–1.6× multiplier isn't greed — it prices the risk you carry: unbooked weeks, scope that grows, invoices that pay late. Employees get that risk absorbed by an employer. You ARE the employer.

Step 4 — price the project, not the hours

An "8-day" project is a 10-day project after revisions, communication, and deployment — pad the estimate before you multiply. 10 × $795 ≈ $7,950. If your gut just said "I'd have quoted four-something," you've found your 30–50% — the gap nearly everyone discovers the first time they run this.

What to do with the numbers

Write them down as a rate card — floor, standard, rush (+40%), retainer (−10% for committed volume) — plus your decline triggers (budget under floor, "quick favor" scope, payment in exposure). Then stop renegotiating with yourself at quote time. The card decides.
